# How Many Employees Should I Schedule Each Shift at My Bike Shop?

## Direct Answer

You stop guessing and start dividing. The formula is **employees needed for a given shift = that shift's average gross profit / your agreed-upon daily gross-profit-per-rep target.** First, you and your leadership team agree on one number: the daily gross profit an average sales-and-service person should produce selling bikes, fitting riders, writing service tickets, and turning the repair stand - call it **$300 a day**. That is a floor, not a ceiling. A bike shop blends higher-margin service and accessories with thinner-margin complete bikes, so the per-person number lands in the upper-middle. Then you pull each shift's trailing three-to-six-month gross profit. If the Saturday opening shift averages **$1,500** in gross profit, then $1,500 / $300 = **5 people** on the floor and in the workshop that shift. If a slow Tuesday mid averages **$600**, you need **2**. You do that for every day part, then place those shifts against when the customers actually arrive - the weekend ride-buying rush, the after-work tune-up drop-offs, the seasonal spring surge - so the bodies are on the floor when the money is. ## FAQ

**How do I set the daily gross-profit-per-person target for a bike shop?**
Look at your trailing shop-wide gross profit and your current crew headcount, then agree on the honest daily floor an average salesperson or mechanic should produce - most bike-shop operators land somewhere between $250 and $400 a day given the blend of high-margin service and accessories with lower-margin complete bikes. Set it with leadership so it is a shared yardstick, not a number one manager invented, and revisit it once or twice a year.

**Should sales staff and service mechanics carry the same target?**
Often not. Service and accessories carry higher margin per labor hour than complete-bike sales, so many shops set a steady target for the sales floor and a slightly different one for the service bench. The division still works - just run separate targets for sales and service if the margins differ enough to matter, and schedule the bench against drop-off volume.

**What if a shift's gross profit swings a lot week to week?**
Use a trailing three-to-six-month average by day of week to smooth the noise, and schedule to that baseline. A bike shop is deeply seasonal, so build a spring-and-summer baseline and a separate winter baseline rather than averaging across the whole year. For known spikes - the first warm weekend, a local race or charity ride, holiday gift season - add a manual bump on top of the calculated count.

**Why staff to gross profit instead of foot traffic or a fixed headcount?**
Foot traffic and "we've always run three on Saturday" do not pay the labor bill - gross profit does. A floor full of browsers and a floor full of bike-plus-accessory-plus-service buyers look the same on a door counter and very different on a P&L. Tying headcount to gross profit guarantees every scheduled person is covered by real margin.
