# How Many Employees Should I Schedule Each Shift at My Tire Shop?

## Direct Answer

You stop guessing and start dividing. The formula is **employees needed for a given shift = that shift's average gross profit / your agreed-upon daily gross-profit-per-rep target.** First, you and your leadership team agree on one number: the daily gross profit an average tech and counter person should produce mounting tires, running alignments, selling road hazard, and writing up service - call it **$400 a day**. That is a floor, not a ceiling. Tires plus the alignment, balance, and road-hazard attachments carry healthier margins than commodity retail, so the per-person number runs higher. Then you pull each shift's trailing three-to-six-month gross profit. If the Saturday opening shift averages **$2,400** in gross profit, then $2,400 / $400 = **6 people** in the shop and on the counter that shift. If a slow Tuesday mid averages **$1,200**, you need **3**. You do that for every day part, then place those shifts against when the bays actually fill - the early drop-off rush, the lunch-hour walk-ins, the after-work pickups - so the bodies are on the floor when the money is. ## FAQ

**How do I set the daily gross-profit-per-person target for a tire shop?**
Look at your trailing shop-wide gross profit and your current crew headcount, then agree on the honest daily floor an average tech or writer should produce - most tire operators land somewhere between $300 and $550 a day once alignment, balance, and road-hazard attachments are counted. Set it with leadership so it is a shared yardstick, not a number one manager invented, and revisit it once or twice a year.

**Should techs and service writers carry the same target?**
Not always. Service writers often produce higher gross profit per head because they sell the alignment, the road hazard, and the additional services, while techs turn the labor. Many operators set a slightly higher target for writers and a steady one for techs - the division still works, just run separate targets for the front counter and the bays if the margins differ enough to matter.

**What if a shift's gross profit swings a lot week to week?**
Use a trailing three-to-six-month average by day of week to smooth the noise, and schedule to that baseline. For known spikes - the first snow that triggers winter-tire demand, a spring road-trip season, a local sales event - add a manual bump on top of the calculated count rather than letting one wild week distort the whole average.

**Why staff to gross profit instead of car count or a fixed headcount?**
Car count and "we've always run four on Saturday" do not pay the labor bill - gross profit does. A bay full of cheap tire-only jobs and a bay full of four-tire-plus-alignment-plus-road-hazard tickets look the same on a car-count board and very different on a P&L. Tying headcount to gross profit guarantees every scheduled person is covered by real margin.
